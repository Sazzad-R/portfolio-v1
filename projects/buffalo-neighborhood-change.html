Gentrification and neighborhood change in Buffalo.

Identifying economically vulnerable census tracts, measuring varying intensities of neighborhood change, and examining their socioeconomic correlates in Buffalo, New York.

01 / OVERVIEW

How does neighborhood transformation vary across a post-industrial city?

Buffalo experienced decades of industrial decline, population loss, and uneven disinvestment. More recently, some neighborhoods have seen reinvestment alongside rapid growth in housing values. This study investigates where these changes are concentrated and how initial socioeconomic conditions relate to their intensity.

Rather than labeling every census tract as either “gentrified” or “not gentrified,” it uses an eligibility screen followed by a continuous index. The focus is on spatially uneven economic and demographic change, not on claiming that the observed changes establish displacement.

Research questions

  1. Which Buffalo census tracts were economically eligible for gentrification between 2016 and 2024?
  2. How intense was socioeconomic change in the eligible tracts?
  3. Which baseline socioeconomic and spatial characteristics were associated with subsequent intensity?
  4. How did housing, income, demographic, and crime indicators differ by intensity group?
02 / METHODS

From geographic data to a continuous intensity index.

01

Assemble tract-level data

Combine 2016 and 2024 American Community Survey estimates with Census TIGER/Line tract boundaries, Buffalo open-data crime and park measures, and municipal assessment and tax information. Match records using geographic identifiers and calculate distance-to-downtown and park coverage.

02

Identify eligible tracts

Classify a tract as economically eligible when its baseline 2016 median household income and home value are below the corresponding citywide medians.

03

Score intensity

Standardize changes in household income, home value, and rent using z-scores; combine the indicators; and standardize the composite to classify low-, medium-, and high-intensity changes among eligible tracts.

04

Model and compare changes

Fit an ordinary least-squares multiple regression with eight predictors, then compare economic and demographic changes across the intensity categories.

03 / RESULTS

Selected findings.

01

Economic starting conditions mattered

Baseline median household income had a negative coefficient and was the only predictor reported as statistically significant at the 1% level in the eight-predictor model. The other modeled characteristics were not statistically significant in this sample.

02

Housing-value changes varied substantially

The study reports average home-value increases of 133.0%, 161.6%, and 257.5% across the low-, medium-, and high-intensity groups, respectively.

03

Demographic change differed across groups

The report documents changes in racial composition, foreign-born residents, and renter population across the intensity categories. These are neighborhood-level patterns, not direct evidence of who moved or why.

Interpretation

The pattern is consistent with reinvestment being associated with initially lower-income neighborhoods, but observational census-tract models cannot establish investor motives, individual residential choices, or the causal impact of gentrification.

05 / INTERPRETATION

What the findings do — and do not — establish.

The study supports a spatially specific description of neighborhood transformation and identifies a strong association between lower baseline income and later index values. However, it does not identify direct causal mechanisms or demonstrate involuntary displacement.

  • Ecological scope: census tract summaries describe aggregate conditions, not household trajectories.
  • Observational design: relationships with the composite index should not be interpreted as causal effects.
  • Model size: the regression uses 28 eligible tracts and eight predictors, so coefficient stability and generalizability warrant caution.
  • Next research step: longitudinal household data, qualitative interviews, and institutional analysis could help distinguish residential mobility, displacement, and other mechanisms.
Research trajectory

This project helped shape my interest in moving beyond spatial patterns to examine the household decisions, constraints, housing markets, and institutions that produce them.
